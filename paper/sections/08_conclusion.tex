\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether survey respondents and LLMs mean the same thing by probability words, and whether it matters for a
crisis-line bot that puts urgency into words.
A citizen codebook built with an adaptation of CWW word encoding shows that respondents agree on extreme phrases and
disagree, mostly between demographic groups, on phrases in the middle (RQ1).
Four local LLMs agree with respondents only partly: they use most phrases more narrowly, often collapse a phrase to one
number, and diverge most for extreme and negative phrases, which reverses our hypothesis about hedge words (RQ2).
In a crisis-line \Perc{}, replacing the citizen codebook with the LLM codebook changes the action for
\WthreeNFlips{} of \WthreeNItems{} help-seeking messages, mostly towards suggesting a helpline less often (RQ3).
The effect is small but systematic, and part of it comes from a gap in the LLM codebook where no level word applies.
We conclude that the meaning of risk words is a design property of an AI-supported public service, and that it should be
defined with the people the service serves and checked against the system, which the word-meaning lab makes concrete.

Future work should collect interval data from people in the service's region and languages, so that the IA or EIA can
encode individual uncertainty, and should include counsellors of crisis lines.
It should test larger and commercial models, and whether giving a model the citizen codebook in its prompt aligns its
meanings.
The rule table needs validation with crisis professionals, and the word-meaning lab a study with its intended users.
