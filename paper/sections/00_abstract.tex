AI chatbots of public services now judge the urgency of help-seeking messages, a judgement naturally expressed in
probability words such as \word{likely}.
We ask whether survey respondents and large language models (LLMs) mean the same thing by these words, and whether it matters.
Using Computing with Words, we encode a citizen codebook of interval type-2 fuzzy sets from \WoneNRespondents{}
respondents and an LLM codebook from \WtwoAnswersTotalNRows{} answers of four local models, and insert both into a
crisis-line Perceptual Computer.
LLM meanings overlap only partly with respondents': they are narrower for three of four models, often collapse to
one number, and diverge most for extreme and negative phrases, reversing our hypothesis on hedge words.
On \WthreeNItems{} real help-seeking messages, the LLM codebook changes the action for a share of
\WthreeActionFlipRateLlmVsCitizenFull{}, mostly by suggesting a helpline less often.
We argue for codebook governance in AI-supported public services.
